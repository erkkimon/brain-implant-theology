# The theology implant

## The split, and why it is real

Apologetics defends the faith against those outside it. Theology argues inside
the faith, between people who already share the scripture they are arguing
from. The opponent is different, so the evidence that counts is different: an
apologetics argument may appeal to history, archaeology or philosophy that a
non-believer accepts, while a theological argument is settled — when it is
settled at all — by texts both sides already hold as authoritative.

That difference is why these are two implants rather than one. See
[the scope convention](../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md) for the
boundary as a rule.

## Scripture is the spine

Everything here hangs off the text. A doctrine is interesting because of the
chapters it rests on; a theologian is interesting because of what they made of
those chapters. So the Bible is not a reference appendix to this brain — it is
the index the rest attaches to, held at chapter granularity, with every verse
individually addressable.

The retrieval mechanics for that are in
[addressing scripture](../conventions/addressing-scripture.md), and they are
not incidental: get the addressing wrong and the entire structure becomes
unsearchable in the one direction that matters most.

## Neutrality is the load-bearing wall

A repository holding Orthodox, Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Pentecostal,
Adventist and Episcopal positions on the same contested questions only works
if it never takes a side. The rule is narrow and strict: we claim that somebody
*said* something, and we prove that. Whether they were right is not ours to
assert.

This is not diplomatic vagueness. It is a stronger epistemic position than
advocacy: the claim "Calvin taught X, here is the passage" is checkable and
either true or false, while "Calvin was right about X" is neither.

## Where it grows

Outward from the text, never thinner. Chapters first, then the traditions'
readings of them, then the theologians who shaped those readings, then the
councils and confessions that fixed them. Ecumenical concepts — where
traditions converge and the precise point at which they part — are first-class
material, because that point of divergence is usually the most informative
thing on the page.
