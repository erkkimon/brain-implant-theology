# Mary Magdalene — one woman, two, or three?

Mary Magdalene is named at the empty tomb by all four gospels and at the cross by Matthew, Mark and John, and in Matthew, Mark (in the longer ending) and John she is a first witness of the risen Jesus. Christians have never disagreed about that. They have disagreed since at least the fourth century about **who else she is**: whether the "sinner" who anoints Jesus's feet in Luke 7 and Mary of Bethany, sister of Martha and Lazarus, are the same woman.

This page reports the positions and where they part. It follows [reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md): the claims are that named people and churches said things, each with its receipt, not that any of them is right.

## What the texts say

Quoted from the stored KJV chapters (public domain); each token below resolves to its chapter file, which also carries the Greek (`TR`, `WH`) and Finnish (`PR1938`) text.

* **Luke 8:2** (`luk8v2`, [chapter](../scripture/luk008.md)): among the women who accompanied Jesus, "Mary called Magdalene, out of whom went seven devils". The verse names a healing from evil spirits and says nothing about sexual sin.
* **Luke 7:37** (`luk7v37`, [chapter](../scripture/luk007.md)): "a woman in the city, which was a sinner" brings an alabaster box of ointment. She is not named in Luke 7.
* **Luke 10:39** (`luk10v39`, [chapter](../scripture/luk010.md)): Martha "had a sister called Mary, which also sat at Jesus' feet". Luke gives no town and no surname.
* **John 11:2** (`jhn11v2`, [chapter](../scripture/jhn011.md)): "It was that Mary which anointed the Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was sick." John 12:3 (`jhn12v3`, [chapter](../scripture/jhn012.md)) then narrates that anointing, at Bethany, by Mary.
* **At the cross and tomb**: John 19:25 (`jhn19v25`, [chapter](../scripture/jhn019.md)), 20:1 (`jhn20v1`, [chapter](../scripture/jhn020.md)), Matthew 28:1 (`mat28v1`, [chapter](../scripture/mat028.md)), Luke 24:10 (`luk24v10`, [chapter](../scripture/luk024.md)).
* **The commission**: John 20:17–18 (`jhn20v17`, `jhn20v18`): Jesus tells her to go to his brethren, and she "came and told the disciples that she had seen the Lord".
* **Mark 16:9** (`mrk16v9`, [chapter](../scripture/mrk016.md)): "he appeared first to Mary Magdalene, out of whom he had cast seven devils". The stored Westcott–Hort text prints this verse inside `⟦ ⟧`, the editors' double brackets for passages they judged later additions; the `TR` section has it as ordinary text. Which witnesses the verse rests on is a separate question from this page's.

## Where the positions part

The disagreement is not about any single verse. It turns on **two inferences** that no gospel states outright:

1. **Is the Luke 7 sinner the same as Mary of Bethany?** Luke 7 names no woman, and John 11:2 describes Mary of Bethany by an anointing that John himself narrates only in chapter 12.
2. **Is Mary of Bethany the same as Mary Magdalene?** The gospels never join the names. The case for it is built from the shared anointing and from Mary's absence from the cross if she is not the Magdalene.

Luke 8:2 follows immediately after Luke 7:36–50 in the narrative, and the seven demons are the only biographical detail the gospels give for the Magdalene. Gregory's argument (below) reads that adjacency as identity; the opposing readings treat it as a coincidence of position.

### The Greek Fathers: three women

The 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports that "the Greek Fathers, as a whole, distinguish the three persons": the sinner of Luke 7, Mary of Bethany, and Mary Magdalene ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "St. Mary Magdalen" (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09761a.htm)). On the later Eastern Orthodox position, the Wikipedia article at a pinned revision states that the Orthodox Church "has always viewed Mary Magdalene as a virtuous Myrrhbearer and 'Equal to the Apostles', distinct from other biblical women" ([Wikipedia, "Mary Magdalene", revision 1377325707](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene&oldid=1377325707)). That is a secondary summary; a primary Orthodox source (the Synaxarion or the Prologue) is not yet stored here.

### The Latin West: one woman

The same encyclopedia says "most of the Latins hold that these three were one and the same" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09761a.htm)). The decisive text is Pope Gregory I's Homily 33 on the gospels, preached about 591, which says of the Luke 7 woman "whom John calls Mary, we believe to be the Mary from whom seven devils were ejected according to Mark", and reads the seven demons as "all the vices" ([quoted with the 591 date in Wikipedia, revision 1377325707](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene&oldid=1377325707)). The sexual reading of the "sinner", and with it the later image of a repentant prostitute, is traced in that article to this homily; the gospels themselves do not call any of the three women a prostitute.

The Catholic Encyclopedia itself argues the Latin case in detail, and concedes its limit: "It is impossible to demonstrate the identity of the three" ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09761a.htm)). Its argument is that John 11:2 uses the Greek `αλειψασα` ("she that anointed") before the anointing John narrates, that Mary of Bethany would otherwise have no place at the cross, and that John calls the woman at the tomb "Mary Magdalene" in 19:25, 20:1 and 20:18 and simply "Mary" in 20:11 and 20:16.

### Earlier Western voices, and one dissent

Per the same Wikipedia article, Ambrose rejected the conflation and Augustine allowed that Mary of Bethany and the Luke 7 sinner might be one without joining either to the Magdalene; so the Latin identification was not unanimous before Gregory ([Wikipedia, revision 1377325707](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene&oldid=1377325707)). Those are summaries of the Fathers' views; the primary passages are not yet stored here.

### The Reformation

In 1517 Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples published a disputation arguing that the three women are distinct, and in 1521 the Sorbonne's theology faculty formally condemned the view as heretical; the same article reports that Luther and Zwingli both supported the composite Magdalene ([Wikipedia, revision 1377325707](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene&oldid=1377325707)). Protestant critics are described by the Catholic Encyclopedia as believing there were "two, if not three, distinct persons", and as contesting above all the identification of Mary of Bethany with the Luke 7 sinner ([Catholic Encyclopedia (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09761a.htm)). That encyclopedia also names Westcott's note on John 11:1 as asserting that the identity of Mary of Bethany with the Magdalene "is a mere conjecture".

### The Roman Catholic Church since 2016

On 10 June 2016 the Congregation for Divine Worship, by decree, raised the 22 July memorial of St Mary Magdalene to a feast, "like that of the other apostles", with a new preface titled *de apostolorum apostola* — "Apostle of the apostles" ([Holy See Press Office, 10 June 2016](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2016/06/10/160610b.html)). The preface's wording rests on the Magdalene's own gospel appearances at the cross, the tomb and the garden, and it says nothing of the Luke 7 woman. The decree does not take a position on the identity question; what it shows is which verses the Church now builds her liturgical honour on.

### Anglican and Lutheran practice

The same Wikipedia revision reports that the 1552 Book of Common Prayer dropped her feast, restored only after some four centuries, and that the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America keeps 22 July as a lesser festival honouring her as an apostle ([Wikipedia, revision 1377325707](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene&oldid=1377325707)). Those are practices, not statements on the identity question.

## What is not on this page

* The apocryphal "Mary" texts (the *Gospel of Mary*, the *Gospel of Philip*, the *Pistis Sophia* and others) portray her as a visionary leader. They sit outside the scriptural spine of this implant, and a page on them will cite and link to the canonical texts above, never the other way round. The Wikipedia article reports that scholarship does not treat them as reliable sources about her life.
* The legend of her later life (Ephesus with the Virgin Mary, or Provence). The Catholic Encyclopedia gives both and the relics' history; it is neither confirmed nor excluded by the gospels.
* Any verdict. A reader who needs one has the verses above and the two inferences that carry the argument.
